Beyond the Forecast

What environmental factors make Seattle weather feel hard for campus life?

Seattle weather is not just rain.

A standard forecast gives us useful numbers: temperature, precipitation, cloud cover, wind speed, sunrise time, and sunset time. But those numbers do not always explain how a day actually feels when students walk across campus, wait for buses, attend evening classes, or move between indoor and outdoor spaces.

This interactive article explores the gap between forecast numbers and lived campus experience. Instead of creating one universal weather difficulty score, we show the environmental layers that shape a Seattle campus day.

Opening Question
Seattle weather is often described as “rainy,” but rain alone does not explain why some campus days feel harder than others.

This project asks: what environmental factors make Seattle weather feel hard?
1. Beyond the Forecast: Weather Layers
A standard forecast gives us separate numbers: temperature, precipitation, cloud cover, wind speed, sunrise time, and sunset time. These numbers are useful, but they do not always explain how a day feels when students move through campus.

This first visualization separates the forecast into six environmental layers: rain, cloud cover, daylight, solar energy, wind, and temperature comfort. Temperature is treated differently because it does not follow a simple “more is worse” rule. Both cold and hot days can affect campus routines.

The goal is not to create a score. Instead, this view makes the layers visible before we look at yearly patterns, monthly profiles, and weekly interactions.
2. Factor Patterns Across the Year
After introducing the six weather layers, this view lets readers look at one layer at a time across 2025. Instead of combining everything into one score, the line chart shows the seasonal pattern of the selected factor.

Most factors are shown with their original units, such as precipitation in inches, cloud cover in percent, daylight in hours, solar energy in MJ/m², and wind speed in mph. This makes the data easier to trust because readers can see what is actually being measured.

Temperature is handled differently. A higher temperature is not automatically worse, and people experience temperature differently. For this reason, the temperature view shows temperature comfort days: the number of days in a month when the feels-like temperature falls outside our prototype comfort range. This keeps the visualization from treating temperature as a simple “more is worse” variable.
3. Monthly Weather Layer Profile
After looking at one factor across the whole year, this view zooms into one month and compares all six layers side by side. Instead of mixing different units into one score, the main bars show flagged days: how many days in that month crossed each layer’s prototype threshold.

This choice makes the comparison more honest. Rain is measured in inches, cloud cover is measured in percent, daylight is measured in hours, and wind is measured in mph. Temperature is even more different because it depends on comfort range. Counting flagged days gives all six layers a shared scale: how often each condition became noticeable in that month.

The smaller labels still show the monthly average value, so readers can connect the flagged-day pattern back to the original weather data.
4. Build Your Weekly Weather Lens
The monthly profile shows which layers appear often, but students do not experience weather as a whole month at once. They experience it through specific weeks: walking to class, waiting for buses, attending evening courses, or moving between buildings.

This view lets readers choose the layers that matter most to their own campus routine. For example, a commuter may care more about rain and wind, while a student with evening classes may pay more attention to daylight. Someone sensitive to gray weather may focus on cloud cover and solar energy.

Darker days do not mean “worse” days. They mean more of the selected layers are present on that day. This keeps the interaction personal and transparent without creating one universal weather score.
5. Day in Context
After selecting a day from the weekly lens, users can inspect the raw weather values behind it. This view shows rain amount, cloud cover, daylight duration, solar energy, wind speed, and feels-like temperature.

Instead of giving the day a final score, this view explains why the day may feel hard through visible weather layers and actual data values.
Takeaway
This project does not create one universal weather difficulty score. Instead, it keeps the factors visible and helps users understand how weather experience is layered, contextual, and personal.

Final Takeaway

The main goal of this project is to help users move beyond a simple “Seattle is rainy” explanation. By separating and reconnecting different weather layers, the article helps students understand why some days feel harder than the forecast suggests.
