Beyond the Forecast

What environmental factors make Seattle weather feel hard for campus life?

Seattle weather is not just rain.

A standard forecast gives us useful numbers: temperature, precipitation, cloud cover, wind speed, sunrise time, and sunset time. But those numbers do not always explain how a day actually feels when students walk across campus, wait for buses, attend evening classes, or move between indoor and outdoor spaces.

This interactive article explores the gap between forecast numbers and lived campus experience. Instead of creating one universal weather difficulty score, we show the environmental layers that shape a Seattle campus day.

Opening Question
Seattle weather is often described as “rainy,” but rain alone does not explain why some campus days feel harder than others.

This project asks: what environmental factors make Seattle weather feel hard?
1. Beyond the Forecast: Weather Layers
A standard forecast gives us separate numbers: temperature, precipitation, cloud cover, wind speed, sunrise time, and sunset time. These numbers are useful, but they do not always explain how a day feels when students move through campus.

This first visualization separates the forecast into six environmental layers: rain, cloud cover, daylight, solar energy, wind, and temperature comfort. Temperature is treated differently because it does not follow a simple “more is worse” rule. Both cold and hot days can affect campus routines.

The goal is not to create a score. Instead, this view makes the layers visible before we look at yearly patterns, monthly profiles, and weekly interactions.
2. Factor Patterns Across the Year
Before comparing many factors together, this view lets readers focus on one environmental layer at a time. Users can choose rain, cloud cover, daylight, solar energy, wind, or temperature comfort, and then see how that factor changes across 2025.

This matters because each factor has its own seasonal rhythm. Rain and cloud cover may be more noticeable in colder months, while daylight and solar energy change more gradually across the year. By separating the factors first, readers can see that Seattle weather is not shaped by one condition alone.

This view works as the bridge between the opening idea and the later comparison views. It helps users understand the yearly pattern before asking how multiple conditions overlap within a month or week.
3. Monthly Weather Layer Profile
A month can feel difficult for different reasons, but directly comparing raw weather values would be misleading because each factor uses a different unit. Rain is measured in inches, cloud cover is a percentage, daylight is measured in hours, and wind is measured in miles per hour.

In this view, each bar shows the selected month’s relative intensity within that factor’s yearly range. For example, a taller rain bar means that this month is rainier compared with other months in 2025, not that rain uses the same scale as daylight or wind.

The label below each bar also shows flagged days. A flagged day means that the factor crossed our prototype experience threshold. This separates two ideas: how strong a factor is on average, and how often it appears during the month.
4. Build Your Weekly Weather Lens
In this view, users choose which weather layers matter most to their own campus routine. A student who walks across campus may care more about rain and wind, while a student with evening classes may care more about daylight.

When selected conditions appear on the same day, the day becomes darker. This shows how weather layers overlap without creating one fixed weather difficulty score.
5. Day in Context
After selecting a day from the weekly lens, users can inspect the raw weather values behind it. This view shows rain amount, cloud cover, daylight duration, solar energy, wind speed, and feels-like temperature.

Instead of giving the day a final score, this view explains why the day may feel hard through visible weather layers and actual data values.
Takeaway
This project does not create one universal weather difficulty score. Instead, it keeps the factors visible and helps users understand how weather experience is layered, contextual, and personal.

Final Takeaway

The main goal of this project is to help users move beyond a simple “Seattle is rainy” explanation. By separating and reconnecting different weather layers, the article helps students understand why some days feel harder than the forecast suggests.
