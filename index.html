A visual article about the weather layers that shape everyday campus life in Seattle.

Beyond the Forecast

IMT 561 Final Project · Spring 2026 · Kexuan Feng & Lexi He · ~8 min read

Seattle weather is not just rain.

A forecast is useful, but it rarely captures how a day feels once someone is walking across campus, waiting for a bus, or leaving an evening class. In Seattle, a hard weather day often comes from several ordinary conditions happening together: wet ground, low light, cloud cover, wind, and temperature that feels colder or warmer than expected.

Campus scenario

Imagine a student leaving class in Seattle. The forecast says 55°F, light rain, and cloudy skies. But the day may feel harder than those numbers suggest: the sidewalk is wet, the sky is gray, daylight is short, and the wind makes waiting outside feel colder.

This article is designed for students, commuters, and campus walkers who need to understand not only what the weather is, but how different weather layers combine into the feeling of a school day.

Opening Roadmap
A forecast usually arrives as a list of numbers: temperature, rain, cloud cover, wind, sunrise, and sunset. On campus, those numbers rarely stay separate. They combine into the feeling of walking between buildings, waiting for a bus, or leaving class after dark.

This roadmap follows that shift: from forecast numbers, to weather layers, to the context of a year, a month, a week, and one specific day.
Beyond the Forecast: Weather Layers
Seattle weather is often described through rain, but rain is only one part of the experience. A cloudy, windy, short-day afternoon can feel difficult even when precipitation is low.

The first visualization separates one forecast into six layers: rain, cloud cover, daylight, solar energy, wind, and temperature comfort. Temperature is treated differently because higher is not always worse. Cold days and hot days can both affect how comfortable campus movement feels.

The point is not to rank the day. It is to make the hidden layers of an ordinary forecast easier to see.

How to read Start with the forecast card, then follow the arrow into the weather-layer list. A layer is marked present when that day crosses the prototype threshold.

Source: Visual Crossing Seattle daily weather data, 2025. Thresholds are prototype design choices.

Factor Patterns Across the Year
Looking at the year one factor at a time makes Seattle’s weather feel less like a stereotype and more like a seasonal pattern. Rain, daylight, cloud cover, and solar energy do not move in the same way.

Each line keeps its original unit, such as inches of precipitation, percent cloud cover, hours of daylight, or miles per hour of wind. Keeping the units visible helps the chart stay close to the actual weather data.

Temperature is shown as comfort days rather than average temperature. A month rises when more days fall outside the prototype comfort range, instead of when the weather is simply warmer.

How to read Choose one factor at a time. The line shows the monthly pattern across 2025, using the selected factor’s own unit instead of a combined score.

Source: 2025 Seattle daily weather data, aggregated by month.

Monthly Weather Layer Profile
A month can feel heavy for different reasons. One month may have many rainy days; another may stand out because of cloud cover, short daylight, or low solar energy.

To compare these layers fairly, the chart counts flagged days: days when a layer crosses its prototype threshold. This avoids mixing inches, percentages, hours, and temperature into one unclear score.

The smaller labels keep the original monthly averages nearby, so the flagged-day pattern can still be traced back to the weather values behind it.

How to read Select a month and compare how often each layer was flagged. The bars use flagged-day counts so different units can be compared on the same scale.

Method note: Flagged days are counted from prototype thresholds for each weather layer.

Build Your Weekly Weather Lens
Students do not experience weather as a monthly average. They experience it through specific routines: walking across campus, waiting outside, commuting, or leaving an evening class.

The weekly lens lets the reader choose which layers matter most to that routine. A commuter may care most about rain and wind. Someone with late classes may pay more attention to daylight. Someone sensitive to gray weather may focus on cloud cover and solar energy.

Darker bars do not mean objectively worse days. They mean more of the selected layers are present at the same time.

How to read Choose the layers that matter most to a campus routine. Darker stacked bars mean more of the selected layers were present on that day.

Method note: The weekly lens is personal; it does not rank days as universally better or worse.

Day in Context: Check the Raw Weather
The final view returns to one day. After a pattern is highlighted, the raw values show what actually produced that label.

This is important because color alone should not carry the argument. Each selected day shows precipitation, cloud cover, daylight hours, solar energy, wind speed, and feels-like temperature.

Temperature remains a comfort layer. A day is flagged only when the feels-like temperature falls outside the prototype range, not because the number is simply high or low.

How to read Use the arrows to inspect one day from the selected week. The raw values explain why each layer was marked present or absent.

Method note: Temperature is treated as comfort, so it is flagged only when feels-like temperature falls outside the prototype range.

Coda

Beyond the Forecast

Seattle weather is often described as rain, but the harder campus days are rarely explained by rain alone. Across the year, the story becomes clearer when rain, cloud cover, daylight, solar energy, wind, and temperature comfort are read together.

This article does not create one final weather score. Instead, it offers a way to inspect the layers behind a forecast and ask why a day may feel harder for students moving through campus.

Methodology

This project uses 2025 Seattle daily weather data and derives six weather layers from precipitation, cloud cover, daylight hours, solar energy, wind speed, and feels-like temperature. The thresholds are prototype design choices for exploring campus weather experience.

Limitations

The visualization does not measure individual mood or actual student behavior. It shows environmental conditions that may shape campus experience, so it should be read as an exploratory lens rather than a universal judgment of the weather.

How Layers Were Flagged

Each weather layer was translated into a simple present / absent signal. These thresholds are not universal comfort standards. They are prototype rules used to make the weather conditions easier to compare across days.

Rain

Flagged when daily precipitation is present.

Cloud cover

Flagged when cloud cover is high enough to shape the visual feel of the day.

Daylight

Flagged when daylight hours are short.

Solar energy

Flagged when solar energy is low.

Wind

Flagged when wind speed may affect walking, waiting, or outdoor comfort.

Temperature comfort

Flagged when feels-like temperature falls outside the prototype comfort range.
