Beyond the Forecast

A visual article about the weather layers that shape everyday campus life in Seattle.

Beyond the Forecast

IMT 561 Final Project · Spring 2026 · Kexuan Feng & Lexi He · ~8 min read

Seattle weather is not just rain.

A forecast is useful, but it rarely captures how a day feels once someone is walking across campus, waiting for a bus, or leaving an evening class. In Seattle, a hard weather day often comes from several ordinary conditions happening together: wet ground, low light, cloud cover, wind, and temperature that feels colder or warmer than expected.

Campus scenario

Imagine checking the forecast before class: 55°F, light rain, cloudy. On paper, that sounds manageable. Outside, the same day can feel different. The sky stays gray, the sidewalk is wet, the daylight fades early, and the wind makes a short wait at the bus stop feel longer.

This article uses 2025 Seattle daily weather data to pull those conditions apart. Instead of treating “bad weather” as one score, it follows six layers that shape the campus experience: rain, cloud cover, daylight, solar energy, wind, and temperature comfort.

Opening Roadmap
This article does not rank Seattle days with a single “bad weather” score. It starts with the basic forecast variables, then keeps them separate long enough to see what each one contributes.

As you scroll, the view moves from a single forecast, to yearly patterns, to monthly comparisons, to a selected week, and finally to one day’s raw weather values.
Beyond the Forecast: Weather Layers
Weather apps list numbers one by one: temperature, precipitation, cloud cover, wind speed, sunrise, and sunset. On campus, those numbers are not experienced one by one. They arrive together.

This first view separates the forecast into six layers: rain, cloud cover, daylight, solar energy, wind, and temperature comfort. Temperature needs its own treatment because higher is not automatically worse. A cold morning and an unusually warm afternoon can both change how a day feels.

The point is to make the layers visible before comparing them across the year, across a month, and across one lived week.
Factor Patterns Across the Year
Once the layers are separated, seasonal patterns become easier to read. Choose one factor at a time and follow how it changes through 2025.

Most layers stay in their original units: precipitation in inches, cloud cover in percent, daylight in hours, solar energy in MJ/m², and wind speed in mph. Keeping the units visible makes the chart less like a black-box score and more like an article you can check against the data.

Temperature is handled as comfort days. A month rises in this view when more days fall outside the prototype comfort range, not simply when the average temperature is higher.
Monthly Weather Layer Profile
The yearly view shows one layer at a time. This view zooms into one month and puts all six layers next to each other.

Because the variables use different units, the main bars count flagged days: days when a layer crossed its prototype threshold. That shared scale makes the comparison more readable without pretending rain, daylight, wind, and temperature are the same kind of measurement.

The smaller labels still show the monthly averages, so the summary stays tied to the original weather values.
Build Your Weekly Weather Lens
A month can reveal a pattern, but students usually feel weather through smaller routines: a Monday commute, a walk between buildings, a late class, or a wait outside after dark.

This view lets readers choose the layers that matter most to their own routine. A commuter might turn on rain and wind. A student with evening classes might focus on daylight. Someone affected by gray days might look more closely at cloud cover and solar energy.

Darker days are not labeled as universally worse. They simply show that more of the selected layers were present on that day.
Day in Context: Check the Raw Weather
The final view returns to one day. After seeing yearly, monthly, and weekly summaries, this panel shows the actual weather values behind the visual labels.

This is important because the article should not ask readers to trust color alone. For the selected day, the view shows precipitation, cloud cover, daylight hours, solar energy, wind speed, and feels-like temperature.

Temperature remains a comfort measure. A day is flagged only when the feels-like temperature falls outside the prototype comfort range.

Coda

Beyond the Forecast

Seattle weather is often described as rain, but the harder campus days are rarely explained by rain alone. Across the year, the story becomes clearer when rain, cloud cover, daylight, solar energy, wind, and temperature comfort are read together.

This article does not create one final weather score. Instead, it offers a way to inspect the layers behind a forecast and ask why a day may feel harder for students moving through campus.

Methodology

This project uses 2025 Seattle daily weather data and derives six weather layers from precipitation, cloud cover, daylight hours, solar energy, wind speed, and feels-like temperature. The thresholds are prototype design choices for exploring campus weather experience.

Limitations

The visualization does not measure individual mood or actual student behavior. It shows environmental conditions that may shape campus experience, so it should be read as an exploratory lens rather than a universal judgment of the weather.
